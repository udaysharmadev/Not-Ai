# Linguistic profile: use as context, not a target

Read this reference when a passage feels consistently dense or mismatched to
its genre and the reason is not obvious from vocabulary alone.

Reinhart et al. compared human continuations with outputs from GPT-4o and Llama
3 variants across several English registers. Instruction-tuned models used some
features at different population rates, including present participial clauses,
nominalizations, phrasal coordination, passive voice, and stance markers.

These findings support one editorial question: **does this passage use the
grammar its situation needs?** They do not provide ceilings, floors, or a recipe
for rewriting an individual document.

## Questions worth asking

- Does a personal note carry the density of an academic abstract?
- Does an academic abstract lose necessary precision when made conversational?
- Do several nouns hide the actor or action the reader needs?
- Does a participial clause express a real relationship, or merely attach a
  polished-sounding summary?
- Is passive voice useful because the actor is unknown or irrelevant?
- Does the writer qualify a claim at the level the evidence supports?

## Decisions the evidence does not justify

- Do not insert `because`, contractions, first person, passive voice, or short
  sentences to hit a frequency.
- Do not replace a precise technical term merely because it is long or Latinate.
- Do not treat a feature count as evidence about who wrote the passage.
- Do not compare the bundled regex proxies with rates produced by a parsed
  research corpus. They are different measurements.

The correct comparison is usually local: the draft against its purpose, genre,
source material, and, when supplied with consent, the same writer's work.
