Our team built and tested a low-cost air-quality logging station for the civil engineering workshop at IIT Roorkee between 14 January 2026 and 28 February 2026. We compared three Plantower PMS5003 optical sensors against the roof-mounted reference gravimetric sampler located 12 meters away, logging PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations every 60 seconds to a local SQLite database on a Raspberry Pi 4B. Because winter fog events in late January pushed relative humidity above 85% on nineteen mornings, the uncorrected optical scattering counts read substantially higher than the dry gravimetric filter reference during morning hours.

When we inspected the raw minute-by-minute time series, we found that Sensor B drifted upward by a systematic offset of 11.2 ug/m3 whenever ambient relative humidity rose above 80%. To correct that humidity bias, we wired a co-located SHT31 temperature-and-humidity probe to the logger and fit a two-parameter Kohler-style hygroscopic correction across the full 45-day dataset. Applying that humidity correction reduced the root-mean-square error (RMSE) between the three-sensor PMS5003 average and the daily gravimetric reference from 24.8 ug/m3 to 9.6 ug/m3, while the Pearson correlation improved from r = 0.78 to r = 0.93 across the campaign.

However, the humidity model did not fix rapid concentration spikes during the 30-minute courtyard diesel generator tests held every Tuesday morning between 09:00 and 09:30. During those exhaust plumes, the optical sensors saturated above 320 ug/m3 because the small internal intake fan could not clear coarse soot particles before they coated the inner baffle. Blowing out the intake chamber with dry compressed air every 14 days prevented baseline drift throughout February. The project shows that optical particulate sensors can provide reliable workshop exposure logs if paired with local SHT31 humidity compensation and a biweekly maintenance schedule, although they cannot replace reference gravimetric filters for formal regulatory compliance.
