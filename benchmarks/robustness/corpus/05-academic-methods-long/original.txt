To isolate the effect of sentence segmentation from lexical choice, we constructed a balanced corpus of 360 English passages stratified across six registers: undergraduate lab reports, peer-reviewed methods sections, technical documentation, workplace correspondence, reflective essays, and short social posts. Each stratum contained 30 human-authored passages written prior to November 2022 and 30 passages generated by instruction-tuned language models under matched topic prompts. Passages in the short stratum contained between 90 and 140 words (mean = 108.4, SD = 11.7), whereas passages in the long stratum contained between 310 and 480 words (mean = 364.2, SD = 38.5) to satisfy the 300-word minimum required by institutional screening tools such as Turnitin (2024).

For each source passage, we applied six deterministic, word-preserving punctuation transformations while holding the case-folded alphanumeric token sequence strictly invariant. Transformations were verified using exact character opcode diffs and SHA-256 token-stream digests. Non-prosodic periods and commas appearing inside decimal numbers (for example, 3.14 or 1,000), timestamps (09:30 a.m.), uniform resource locators, parenthetical year citations, and standard abbreviations (such as Dr., Fig., or U.S.) were masked prior to transformation so that factual literals remained untouched. We then measured how each perturbation affected rule-based sentence splitters, dependency-parsed clause counts, and byte-pair encoding (BPE) token boundaries near sentence transitions.

Across the short stratum, removing a single internal sentence period reduced the detected sentence count by 12.5% and increased the sample coefficient of variation (CV) of sentence length from a mean of 0.382 to 0.541 (paired median difference = +0.148, IQR = 0.092). In the long stratum, the same single-stop deletion changed sentence-length CV by only +0.039 (IQR = 0.028), demonstrating that short passages are disproportionately sensitive to single-boundary edits. However, blinded human raters (k = 4) marked 91.7% of period-deletion variants in formal registers as grammatically defective run-on sentences, confirming that boundary deletion degrades prose quality even when it inflates statistical variance metrics.
